## Specifying is not picking the biggest number

This is where most UV LED purchases go wrong. A system with more irradiance is not automatically the right system — and an oversized one costs money twice, once at purchase and again in energy.

What actually determines the specification:

- **Your chemistry's absorption spectrum.** Many UV adhesives and coatings are formulated around 365 nm, not 385 or 395 nm. Match the wavelength to the photoinitiator, not to the marketing.
- **Required dose (mJ/cm²) against line speed.** Dose is irradiance multiplied by exposure time. A faster line has less exposure time, so it needs more irradiance for the same dose. This is arithmetic, and it is where the choice between AC8+, AC9+ and AC8+HD is actually settled.
- **Working distance.** Irradiance falls off with distance. The number on the datasheet is measured at a specific distance — the number at your substrate is the one that cures your adhesive.
- **Cure width and substrate contour.** A flat 200 mm web and an irregular three-dimensional part need different head arrangements, even at the same dose.
- **The environment around the head.** Solvent vapour, dust and ambient temperature all affect real service life.

## Life cycle management — the part unique to UV LED

Here is something that catches people migrating from mercury lamps:

> **A mercury lamp fails visibly. A UV LED does not.** It dims slowly, over thousands of hours. The line keeps running, the operators see light, and nothing looks wrong — until a batch fails cure and nobody can say when the drift began.

That single difference is why UV LED equipment needs active life cycle management, and why mercury-era maintenance habits do not transfer. Our approach across the life of a system:

**1 · Process window validation, before purchase.** Confirm the dose and wavelength your chemistry actually requires, so the specification is based on measurement rather than assumption.

**2 · Baseline at commissioning.** Measure and record irradiance at installation with a radiometer. **This baseline is the reference for every judgement that follows** — without it, later readings mean nothing.

**3 · Scheduled measurement.** Periodic readings at the same point, compared against the baseline, turn invisible degradation into a visible curve. You see the trend before it becomes a quality problem.

**4 · Managing the decay.** Knowing when closed-loop compensation is enough, when line speed needs adjusting, and when a head genuinely needs replacing — instead of discovering it through scrap.

**5 · Calibration and traceability.** The radiometer itself needs calibration. For medical device manufacturers running IQ/OQ/PQ, a documented, traceable calibration history is not optional — it is audit evidence.

**6 · Spares and upgrade path.** Local availability of the parts that stop a line, and an honest assessment of whether a platform upgrade like this one pays for itself on your specific process — or whether your existing system is fine.

The radiometers matter here as much as the curing systems. The [OmniCure R2000](/product/systems/r2000) and [LS200](/product/systems/ls200) exist precisely because you cannot manage what you do not measure.
